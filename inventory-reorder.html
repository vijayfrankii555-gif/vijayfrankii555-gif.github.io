How to Build an Inventory Reorder Report

A corporate framework for turning stock balances, demand history, lead time and safety stock into an actionable reorder report. The examples use a fictional multi-location distribution dataset.

Important: All products, quantities, prices and business conditions in this article are fictional. The purpose is to demonstrate reporting logic, not to prescribe a single inventory policy for every business.

1. What Does an Inventory Reorder Report Do?

An inventory reorder report helps a business identify items that may need replenishment before available stock reaches an unacceptable level.

A useful report should connect four ideas: current stock, expected demand, replenishment lead time and the agreed safety buffer.

3. The Core Formula: Reorder Point

A common starting point for a reorder report is the Reorder Point (ROP). One practical formula is:

Reorder Point = Average Daily Usage × Lead Time + Safety Stock

For SKU-1001 in the example:

ROP = 30 × 8 + 120 = 360 units

With current stock of 420 units, the item is above the example reorder point. This does not automatically mean that no purchase action is required; an actual decision may also consider open purchase orders, pending transfers, demand changes, minimum order quantity and supplier conditions.

4. Calculate Average Daily Usage

Daily usage should be based on a clearly defined historical period. For example, a 30-day or 90-day period may be used depending on the business cycle.

Average Daily Usage = Total Usage During Period / Number of Days

In Excel, if total usage is in B2 and the number of days is in C2:

=IFERROR(B2/C2,0)

5. Calculate Reorder Point in Excel

Assume these columns exist: F = Avg Daily Usage, G = Lead Time Days, H = Safety Stock.

=F2*G2+H2

The formula is intentionally simple. In a production inventory system, the business rules around demand, seasonality and lead time should be documented separately.

6. Add Available Stock Instead of Looking Only at Physical Stock

A stronger report distinguishes physical stock from stock that is actually available for planning.

Available Stock = On Hand + Confirmed Incoming - Reserved / Allocated Stock

The exact definition should match the company's ERP and inventory policy. Never assume that every unit physically present is available to promise.

7. Calculate Stock Coverage

Stock coverage estimates how many days the current available stock could support the current demand rate.

Stock Cover Days = Available Stock / Average Daily Usage
Example: =IFERROR(I2/F2,0)

Comparing stock cover with supplier lead time gives an additional management signal. For example, an item with 5 days of stock cover and a 12-day lead time deserves review.

8. Create a Reorder Status

Once the key calculations exist, create a transparent status column.

=IF(I2<=J2,"REORDER","NO REORDER")

Here, I2 represents available stock and J2 represents reorder point. The business may add additional rules for open purchase orders or transfers.

9. Add Reorder Priority

Not every exception needs the same urgency. A priority field helps procurement, stores and operations focus on the right items.

=IFS(I2<=J2*0.5,"HIGH",I2<=J2,"MEDIUM",TRUE,"LOW")

The 50% threshold above is only an example. Priority thresholds should be defined by the business based on service levels, lead time and stockout impact.

10. Use SUMIFS for Location-Wise Inventory Reporting

In a multi-location business, management often needs to know total quantity, reorder value or exception count by warehouse or distribution center.

=SUMIFS(Inventory[Current Stock],Inventory[Location],$B2)

You can add more criteria for category, supplier, SKU family or status.

11. Count Reorder Exceptions With COUNTIFS

A management summary can include the number of items requiring action.

=COUNTIFS(Inventory[Reorder Status],"REORDER",Inventory[Location],$B2)

This is useful for a daily or weekly exception dashboard because management can see how many SKUs need attention before opening the detailed report.

17. Important Controls Before Using the Report

Control 01

Confirm that stock balances come from the same reporting time and location scope.

Control 02

Confirm that units of measure are consistent. Do not mix pieces, cartons and kilograms without conversion rules.

Control 03

Document the demand history period used for Average Daily Usage.

Control 04

Document how Safety Stock is calculated or approved.

Control 05

Check open purchase orders and transfers before issuing a new reorder recommendation.

18. Common Inventory Reporting Mistakes

  • Using only current stock and ignoring demand velocity.
  • Using an arbitrary safety buffer with no documented business rule.
  • Ignoring supplier lead time.
  • Counting reserved stock as freely available stock.
  • Ignoring open purchase orders or stock transfers.
  • Mixing units of measure.
  • Using old demand history when the business is changing quickly.
  • Showing a “REORDER” flag without explaining the rule behind it.
  • Publishing confidential stock or supplier information in a public portfolio.

19. Practical Build Sequence

Step 01

Standardize the SKU and item master.

Step 02

Load current stock, reservations and confirmed incoming quantities.

Step 03

Calculate demand rate from an approved historical period.

Step 04

Apply lead time and safety stock to calculate reorder point.

Step 05

Calculate stock cover and exception status.

Step 06

Assign priority using documented thresholds.

Step 07

Summarize exceptions by location, category and product group.

Step 08

Validate the report against the source system before taking procurement action.

20. Final Checklist

  • Is the stock balance current?
  • Are all SKUs using a consistent unit of measure?
  • Is demand history clearly defined?
  • Is lead time documented?
  • Is safety stock based on an approved rule?
  • Does the reorder point calculate correctly?
  • Are open purchase orders and transfers considered?
  • Can a manager see the high-priority exceptions immediately?

14. Colour Formatting Rules for Inventory

Conditional formatting should make the action obvious without opening every calculation.

  • Red: reorder required or stock cover below the critical threshold.
  • Amber: item is approaching the reorder trigger or needs review.
  • Green: healthy stock position based on the defined rule.
  • Use the same thresholds in Excel and Power BI so the two reports tell the same story.

Final Takeaway

A useful reorder report is not just a stock list. It is an exception-management tool that combines inventory position, demand, lead time and business rules. The goal is to make the reason for a reorder decision visible and auditable.

Build the calculation logic first, document the rules, validate the data, and only then design the management dashboard.
